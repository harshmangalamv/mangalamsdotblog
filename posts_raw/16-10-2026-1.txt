Bihar is home to roughly one Indian in twelve, and for most of the last seventy years it has sat at or near the bottom of almost every list that measures how well people live. This series asks a simple question: how did that happen, and why has it been so hard to change?

## The question

Poverty this persistent rarely has a single cause. It is usually a stack of decisions, accidents and habits that reinforce one another. The aim of this first part is to lay out the shape of the problem before arguing about causes.

## What the numbers say

Start with something basic: how many people can read and write. In the 2011 census, Bihar had the lowest literacy rate of any state.[^literacy]

| Region | Literacy rate, 2011 |
|---|--:|
| Kerala | 93.91% |
| India | 74.04% |
| Bihar | 63.82% |

<!-- A table: a header row, then a separator row, then data.

A gap of thirty percentage points with Kerala is not a statistic you can explain with one policy. It reflects decades of differences in schooling, in who got to go to school, and in what a family could afford to give up when a child sat in a classroom instead of working.

### A note on sources

Numbers on Bihar differ by source and by year, and some figures are revised after first publication.[^revisions] Every figure in this series links to where it came from, so you can check it yourself.

<!-- "###" is a smaller heading inside a section. It does not appear in the Contents list. -->

## A policy that tilted the map

One explanation that comes up again and again is the **freight equalisation policy**. From 1952 to 1993, the central government subsidised the transport of minerals so that factories anywhere in India paid roughly the same for them.[^freight] Bihar, which then included the mineral belt that is now Jharkhand, lost the advantage of having those minerals next door.

> The policy is often blamed for keeping industry away from the east. How much it actually explains is the question part 3 will take up.
